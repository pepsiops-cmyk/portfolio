01 · Context

New users were leaving at the front door

The product is a consumer chat platform where users talk to AI chatbots that other users create. The homepage was the catalog: every bot on one page, sorted by what was trending.

Leadership noticed a lot of new users leaving straight from the homepage. They didn't click onboarding or anything else. The brief: redesign the homepage so new users stay.

02 · Problem

One glance made the site look like one kind of bot

New users leaving without a single click meant the first screen was the whole decision. So the question was: what does a new user see in that first glance, and what do they take from it?

1. Trending filled the whole screen

Sorting by trending pushed whatever was popular that day to the top, and it took up the entire first screen. A new user saw a dozen bots of the same genre and assumed that was all the site had. On slow days the trending bots could just be dull.

2. People didn't know what they wanted yet

Beta testers put the deeper problem into words. Looking for a new bot was tiring because they didn't know what they wanted, so they just scanned. A single list only works if you already know what you're looking for. Everyone else has to scan everything, and if the first screen is all one kind of bot, there's nothing to grab onto.

03 · The calls

Let people sample before they commit

Call 1

Rows by genre and topic, not one list

Each row is its own small shelf: trending, then genres and topics. Trending gets one row instead of the whole page. A user who doesn't know what they want can glance down the row titles, notice what catches their eye, and dive into that row.

Why rows
Row titles do the sorting for the user. Instead of judging bots one by one, they first pick a kind of story, which is a much easier choice.
Tradeoff
Fewer bots per genre on the first screen than a full grid would show. Worth it because range is what a new user needs to see first, and each row opens up to show more.

Call 2

Give new bots a row of their own

In one big list, a newly created bot lands at the bottom of the pile. A "New this week" row gives fresh bots a place to be seen, and gives users something different from the usual trending picks.

Tradeoff
New bots haven't proven themselves yet, so some will be weaker than trending ones. Worth it for the variety, and because it's one row, not the whole page.

Call 3

A card where the art leads

The redesign came with a new bot card. The art got taller and takes up more of the card, and the bot's name moved onto the image. The card also got more compact, so more bots fit in each row.

Why
Someone browsing without a goal judges a bot by its picture first. A bigger image gives good art more room to catch their eye, and gives creators a reason to make art worth looking at.
Tradeoff
Smaller text for the description and tags. Worth it because the art and name do the first job, catching attention, and the details only matter once someone is interested.

Call 4

Ship it as its own page first

I recommended putting the rows on the homepage itself, since that's where new users were leaving. Leadership chose to keep the homepage the same for everyone and put the rows on a separate Discover page, then merge the two later, because users tend to dislike sudden change.

Tradeoff
Existing users weren't hit with a sudden change, and the rows got proven in the real product before replacing anything. The cost: new users still land on the old homepage until the merge.

Testers said rows let them figure out what they were into before diving deeper, instead of scanning a list with no idea what to look for.

The homepage stayed as it was, including the old card, and its sort options now work more like a search page for people who already know what they want. The merge into the homepage hasn't happened yet, so new users still land on the old homepage. That's why there's no data yet on whether it brings bounce down.

05 · What I'd do next

Put the rows in front of new users and measure it

The fastest way to test the original goal: send only new users to Discover first, keep the homepage the same for existing users, and compare how many new users click something before leaving. That would answer the bounce question without changing anything for existing users.
